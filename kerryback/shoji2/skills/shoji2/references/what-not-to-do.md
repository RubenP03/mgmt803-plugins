# What not to do

A running catalogue of writing that has had to be taken back off these decks.
Read it before writing slide prose, and check a finished deck against it.

Every example below is real text that was written, shown to the instructor, and
removed. The fix is what replaced it. The file is meant to grow: when something
else gets cut, add it with the same three parts — the pattern, the text, the
replacement.

## What a slide is for

The deck is a factual scaffold the instructor lectures from. Every slide exists
so that they can stand beside it and talk. It carries the facts, the mechanism,
the numbers, the commands, the sequence — the things that have to be right and
have to be visible.

It is never cute and never clever. If the material wants a joke, an aside, a
piece of worldliness, or a line that lands, the instructor supplies that out
loud, in the room, where they can own it and drop it if it is not working. Text
on the screen cannot be taken back and cannot read the room.

So the question for every sentence is not whether it is good writing. It is
whether the instructor needs it on the screen while they talk. If they would say
it better themselves, cut it.

## Why this matters more than style

The worst of these is the knowing aside, and it is worth understanding why,
because the reason generalizes to cases this file does not list.

A knowing aside is a claim to experience. "And people are paid on those numbers"
says, in effect: I have watched managers do this, more than once. Someone who
had spent twenty years consulting could write that clause and it would carry
their authority — it would be a war story, compressed.

Written into a teaching deck by someone who has not, it is borrowed worldliness.
It puts a claim about how organizations behave into the instructor's mouth that
they are not making and did not ask for. The failure is misattribution, not
taste. That is why it cannot be fixed by softening the wording.

The slide carries the analysis. What to add from personal experience is the
instructor's decision, made standing in front of the room, where they can own
it. Everything catalogued below is some version of the same overreach: the deck
telling the audience how to take the material instead of presenting it.

## The knowing aside

A trailing clause that stops describing the mechanism and starts implying
something about people's motives. It reads as a wink at the audience.

> A project can move every number in the management accounts without moving a
> dollar, **and people are paid on those numbers.**

The clause insinuates that managers game the numbers. That may be true and it is
not what the slide is about. If the incentive question is worth teaching, it is
worth a slide, an item, or a question — not a raised eyebrow at the end of a
sentence.

Fixed by deleting the clause. The first half is the whole point and it survives
alone.

## The aphorism

A sentence that exists to sound good rather than to add anything. It comes in
three forms, and the narrow reading of this entry once let two of them through.

The test that catches all three: would the sentence survive as a standalone
quotation, lifted off the slide? If it would, it was written to be quoted, and
it goes.

### The closer

Inverts, repeats, or turns a phrase from earlier in the same card.

> Moving each input alone answers a question nobody asked. **Things go wrong
> together, which is the only way they go badly wrong.**

Fixed: "Moving each input alone holds every other input at its base value.
Things that go wrong tend to go wrong together." Same content, no epigram.

Two more, both closing a valuation slide whose cards already carried the
arithmetic:

> [ROIC 24.3%] [Reinvestment rate 32.9%] [Product 8.0%]
>
> **Growth was never a free assumption. The ratios paid for it.**

> Constant growth and constant ratios mean free cash flow grows at the sales
> rate forever. **The forecast needs one year, not thirty.**

The first was deleted; the three cards state the identity and nothing needs to
be said about it. The second was deleted and the first sentence kept: it says
what is true of the cash flows, where "one year, not thirty" only scores a
point against a forecast nobody had proposed.

### The freestanding maxim

Repeats nothing and therefore escapes the test above. It is a rule of thumb,
pitched as wisdom, usually sitting in a `.note` under a table that already
states it.

> | Right for | your own work | anything you hand to someone else |
>
> ::: note
> **Buy for yourself; build for other people.**
> :::

Deleted. The table row is the content.

### The definitional flip

Restates the slide as an identity, with the two halves swapped for effect.

> [A tool that does not exist] There is nothing to call. No wording reaches it,
> because nothing is being persuaded.
>
> ::: note
> **Everything you can talk it out of was a request, not a constraint.**
> :::

> ::: lead
> **A citation is only worth having if somebody opens it.**
> :::

The first was deleted; the two columns above it carry the distinction. The
second was a `.lead` on a slide whose body is three instructions, and was
replaced with the instruction itself: "Open each passage it cites and check
that the answer is in it."

## The verdict

A ranking or an evaluation stated as though it were a finding. It tells the
audience how to weight something instead of showing them.

> A breakeven turns an argument about beliefs into a question about evidence.
> **It is usually the most useful number in the model.**

> It is one you have not looked at. The single column is not neutral; **it is
> the strongest assumption in the model.**

Fixed by deleting the verdict in the first case, and in the second by replacing
it with what is actually being claimed: "A single column states that every input
is known."

A third, in a `.note` under a terminal value, on a deck whose very next slide
puts the share in a `.stat`:

> Twenty-five times year-5 free cash flow. **A perpetuity at a four-point spread
> is a large multiple, and the terminal value carries most of the answer.**

Fixed: "Twenty-five times year-5 free cash flow, at a four-point spread between
$r$ and $g$." The multiple and the spread are facts about the calculation. How
large a multiple that is, and how much of the answer it carries, is what the
94% on the next slide shows.

## The rhetorical dismissal

Describing a method by sneering at it rather than by saying what it does.

> Moving each input alone **answers a question nobody asked.**

If a technique is inadequate, say what it holds fixed and let the audience draw
the conclusion. Every dismissal is a conclusion the audience should have reached
themselves.

## Meta-commentary on the class

Text about the session rather than about the material: what to notice, how hard
something is, how it relates to the previous hour.

> **Take your time on this one**

> **Twice today.** Charge the project what changes.

> Every number on the screen came from the same case. **Hold that thought.**

> A plant approved in September, and an analyst who rebuilt the model on a
> Sunday. **Read for what she found.**

All deleted. An `.eyebrow` carries a factual locator — a place, a date, a
running time, a source — not a cue about significance.

## Dramatizing vocabulary

Words chosen for temperature rather than accuracy.

> The shortcut is close at 2 percent and **ruinous** at 40.

Fixed: "far off at 40." Also on the list: a lede calling a case "a fight about
who buys it" when it is a disagreement about a cannibalization rate, and one
describing a model as built "on a number nobody was still looking at" when it
was approved by the capital committee in September.

## The emphatic instruction

An imperative that adds volume rather than information.

> Work backward. Take the maximum at each decision date, and carry that back.
> **Never the other way round.**

The first two sentences already say it. Deleted.

## Questions

Two failures, both of which make a question answerable only by someone who
already knows the answer.

**Naming the fact you want.** "Under a headcount freeze, what does the
requisition count actually measure?" Only a student who has worked the case can
answer, and only in the phrasing already in the instructor's head. Replaced
with: what is each of them claiming, and what would have to be true for each to
be right.

**Carrying the answer inside the question.** "Is it 7.0 percent? Why not?" and
"MACRS ignores salvage value. So what is the depreciable basis — $34 million, or
$25 million?" These are quizzes with the answer in the stem.

The form that works across cases: name the item, ask *when* and *how much*.
"Depreciation tax shields: when, how much?" "Floor space: when, how much?" It
scales to every cash flow in a case, it accommodates the items whose honest
answer is nothing, and the disagreements surface on their own when students
compare answers.

## Slide titles

A title that states the finding spends it before anyone has read the slide.

| Written | Replaced with |
|---|---|
| The old machines, and the rebuild that never happens | The old machines, and the rebuild |
| What does not inflate | The other two cash flows |
| Where did the extra value come from? | The mean and the base case |
| The number that ends the argument | Where does that leave the committee? |
| What is the $4 million buying? | Two ways to build it |
| The verdict | NPV and IRR |
| Why capital expenditure is the plug | Capital expenditure as the residual |
| Value this one | Value this company |

## The payoff line

A summary that promises what the audience will have achieved instead of naming
what the block covers.

> Four hands-on blocks. **By six you will have written a skill, reconciled a
> number two ways, and put questions to a dataset nobody in this room holds.**

Two separate failures in one sentence. "Reconciled a number two ways" describes
an activity without naming its subject, so it says nothing about what the
session is about. "A dataset nobody in this room holds" is a flourish standing
where a name belongs.

Fixed: "Four hands-on blocks, about two and a quarter hours: a `CLAUDE.md`, a
skill, the Northwind database, and the EIA database." A session summary lists
the things, and nothing else.

## The elevated noun phrase

A longer, warmer phrase in place of the ordinary word for the thing.

| Written | Replaced with |
|---|---|
| put questions to | query |
| a dataset nobody in this room holds | the EIA database |
| something somebody else can open | a file or an app |
| data you do not hold | an external database |

The plain word is not a compromise. It is the accurate one, and it leaves room
on the slide.

## The metaphor

A figure of speech doing the work of a technical noun.

> When somebody tells you a model can or cannot do something, the useful
> question is **what it was wearing at the time.**

Fixed: "ask which harness it was running in." The metaphor was the only thing
making the sentence memorable, and it was memorable instead of clear.

## The knowing detail

A specific that the writer has no standing to supply. A close relative of the
knowing aside: it borrows the texture of experience rather than its conclusions.

> Typing them into each prompt makes them optional, and one of them gets left
> out **at four in the afternoon.**

Fixed: "and eventually one gets left out." The mechanism is the same; the
invented hour was atmosphere.

## The graded summary

A heading or closer that tells the audience they have learned something, or
grades what just happened.

> **What you just learned about deployment**

> **You spent four days inside a working example of the pattern.**

Fixed: "Deployment, in four lines" and "Your container is an instance of that
pattern." Say what the thing is. Whether it was learned is not the deck's
claim to make.

## The note that restates the slide

A `.note` that adds nothing the body did not already say, usually in a
tighter, more quotable form. It reads as a summing-up and functions as an
epigram.

> | Right for | your own work | anything you hand to someone else |
>
> ::: note
> **Buy for yourself; build for other people.**
> :::

> [A tool that does not exist] There is nothing to call. No wording reaches it,
> because nothing is being persuaded.
>
> ::: note
> **Everything you can talk it out of was a request, not a constraint.**
> :::

Both deleted. A note earns its place by adding a fact, a caveat, a source, or a
pointer to another session — not by phrasing the slide again.

## Padding intensifiers

Words that modulate a claim without changing it.

> That is the shape of **very nearly** everything built on these models.

> Twenty minutes of your own time this week, **and worth it.**

Fixed: "most software built on these models," and "Worth reading." Then check
whether even that survives the test below.

## The test

Read the sentence and ask what it is doing. If it states a fact about the
project, the method, or the tax code, keep it. If it tells the audience how to
feel about that fact, how much it matters, or what kind of people are involved,
cut it. The content shows what matters; saying so does the audience's thinking
for them.
